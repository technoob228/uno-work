# Text for outside readers: write, cut

Based on the principles of *Write, Cut* (Pishi, sokrashchai) by Maxim Ilyakhov and Lyudmila Sarycheva.

Good customer-facing text is honest value for the reader, stated briefly and concretely. We don't push or manipulate: we explain how the product helps and prove it with facts. If the reader doesn't need the product, no text will fix that — and it shouldn't try.

## 0. When to apply and what changes by genre

Always, when the text leaves the team. The cleanup rules (section 3) and honesty apply everywhere. Structure depends on the genre:

| Genre | What matters |
|---|---|
| Landing page, website, proposal, ad | Sales structure (section 2): value → proof → terms → action |
| Presentation, pitch deck | Slide title is a conclusion in a full sentence, not a topic ("Customers leave because of price", not "Churn"). One slide, one idea. Numbers on the slide, not adjectives |
| Email to a customer or partner | The point goes in the first paragraph. One email, one topic. End with what to do and by when. No "Hope this finds you well, I'm reaching out to…" |
| Bad news: price increase, outage, rejection | Fact first and what it means for the reader, then the cause in one sentence, then what we're doing and what they can do. No euphemisms, no excuses |
| Docs, help center, FAQ, how-to | Plain informational style: reader's task → steps (start with a verb) → what they get. No selling, but value and specifics — yes |
| Interface: buttons, errors, empty states, onboarding | Section 5: short, verb first, what happened and what to do |
| Support reply | Answer or fix first, details after. Human language, no templates like "Your call is important to us" |
| Post, newsletter | One idea per post. The first line must be interesting on its own: in a feed, that's all people see |
| Job post | What the person will do, what they'll learn, what it pays, how to apply. No "rockstar ninja" or "fast-paced environment" |

## 1. Before you write, answer four questions

Don't start until you can answer each in one sentence. If the task and project materials don't have the answer, ask. If you can't ask (background task), keep going and leave the missing fact as a visible `[confirm: …]` placeholder.

1. **Who is the reader?** Not "everyone" but a specific person: "an online store owner who sets up WordPress himself".
2. **What is their task or pain?** In the reader's words, not ours.
3. **What do we offer and how does it help them?** The result for them, not a list of our features.
4. **What should they do after reading?** One action: sign up, write to us, buy.

## 2. Sales text structure

This order works almost always. Blocks can shrink to one sentence, but don't reorder them without a reason.

1. **Headline = the main benefit.** What the reader gets. Not a slogan, not wordplay.
2. **The reader's problem or situation.** Briefly, so they recognize themselves.
3. **The offer.** What exactly we give.
4. **Proof.** Numbers, facts, examples, reviews, comparisons. Without these, the rest is just promises.
5. **Terms.** Price, timing, what's included. Hiding the price is the owner's decision, not the writer's.
6. **Remove fears.** What happens if it doesn't fit; how long it takes; whether a card is needed.
7. **Call to action.** One, specific, a verb: "Create a computer", not "Learn more".

## 3. Cleanup rules — apply to every sentence

### Facts instead of judgments
The reader can't check a judgment and won't believe it. Replace it with a fact.

- ❌ "Fast setup" → ✅ "Your computer is ready in 15 seconds"
- ❌ "Robust security" → ✅ "Sign-in with a key only — passwords are never accepted"
- ❌ "Affordable pricing" → ✅ "From $10/month"

### Cut stop words
- **Intensifiers and buzzwords:** very, extremely, unique, innovative, best, high-quality, seamless, powerful, robust, cutting-edge, world-class, best-in-class, revolutionary, game-changing, next-level, unlock, empower.
- **Clichés:** "tailored solutions", "one-stop shop", "team of experts", "wide range of", "end-to-end solution", "take it to the next level".
- **Corporate filler:** "utilize" (use), "leverage" (use), "facilitate" (help), "in order to" (to), "is able to" (can), nominalizations ("perform a verification" → "check", "make a payment" → "pay"), passive voice that hides who does what ("the issue has been resolved" → "we fixed it").
- **Vague quantifiers:** "various", "a number of", "certain", "some", "many customers".
- **Hedges:** "may help", "kind of", "basically", "we strive to".
- **Throat-clearing:** "It's no secret that", "As we all know", "It's worth noting", "In today's fast-paced world".
- **Euphemisms:** "a slight price adjustment" → "the price went up by $2".

### Write about the reader, not about yourself
- ❌ "We are a fast-growing, dynamic company" → ✅ "You get…"
- Count "we/our" and "you/your". If "we" wins, rewrite.

### One idea, one sentence
- Split a long sentence with three clauses into two or three.
- One paragraph, one idea. Three or four lines max.
- Lists for enumerations, tables for comparisons.

### Specifics
- Exact numbers: "312 customers", not "hundreds of customers".
- Real examples: "upload a photo from your phone — and it's already on the site".
- Action verbs: "saves", "sends", "turns on by itself".

### Honesty
- Don't promise what the product doesn't have. If a feature doesn't exist, leave it out or mark it "coming soon".
- Don't invent numbers, reviews, or customers. No data — ask, or write it without a number.
- Don't scare or pressure: no "only 2 spots left" unless true, no fake countdown timers.
- Name downsides and limits yourself — it builds trust.

## 4. Process

1. **Gather facts.** Read the project materials: product description, prices, past copy, brand voice guide if there is one. Write down numbers and proof.
2. **Draft using the structure** from section 2. Meaning first, polish later.
3. **Edit in several passes**, one job per pass:
   - meaning: does each paragraph answer a reader's question? Cut extra blocks;
   - facts: every judgment replaced with a fact or deleted;
   - stop words: go through the list in section 3;
   - rhythm: long sentences split, paragraphs short;
   - out loud: read it as if telling a friend. Where you stumble, rewrite.
4. **Cut again.** A good target is a third of the draft. If a sentence loses nothing without a word, the word goes.

## 5. Interface and short texts

- **Button** — verb + object: "Pay $10", "Create site". Not "OK", "Submit", "Next" when you can be more specific.
- **Screen title** — what happens here or what the user gets.
- **Error** — what happened and what to do: "Card declined. Try another one or contact us."
- **Empty state** — what will be here and how to start.
- **Pricing cards** — 3–4 key specs, no long feature lists.

## 6. Response format

Deliver:
1. **The finished text** — right away, no preamble.
2. **Briefly, what changed** (for edits): 3–5 points like "removed 'fast/robust' — replaced with 15 seconds and key-only sign-in".
3. **What's missing** — facts that would strengthen the text but aren't available: "need a real customer count", "need a review".

If asked for options, give 2–3 **genuinely different** ones (another angle, another main benefit), not reshuffled words.

## Pre-delivery checklist

- [ ] The headline states the reader's benefit
- [ ] No judgment without a fact
- [ ] No stop words from section 3
- [ ] "You" more often than "we"
- [ ] All numbers and promises are true and exist in the product
- [ ] One clear call to action
- [ ] The text is shorter than the draft
